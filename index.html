The Physics of Ski Carving

How does a ski's turning radius change with every turn, and why does a ski turn tighter when you tip it?

A homemade experiment by Vladislav Zalevskiy, with two race skis, some wooden wedges and a pencil.

In 30 seconds

The radius printed on a ski, 12 m on my slalom ski, is the circle its sides are cut from. When a skier tips the ski onto its edge and loads it, the ski bends, and the bend makes it turn tighter: R = Rsc · cos φ, half the printed radius at 60°, where R is the radius of the turn the ski carves, Rsc is the sidecut radius (the printed one) and φ is the edge angle, how far the ski is tipped. I tested this at home on two race skis, and all six measurements agree with the formula to within a few percent.

01

Two ways to turn

There are two ways for a skier to turn. A skid is when the skis glide from side to side across the snow, similar to a car skidding through a corner. The faster it is going, the more snow sprays out, and the more friction, the less speed.

During a carve, the skier turns his skis over on their sharp steel edges, and each edge carves clean and thin, like a train running along its tracks. Racers carve when they can, as a ski that's not sliding sideways is not wasting speed.

This is the site to learn about the carve, and what makes it tight.

In a carve, each segment of the edge is in the same dip in the snow, just like the wheels of a train on the same rails. This is the reason why a carved track is 2 thin lines, one for each ski, while a skid creates a wide smeared line.

Skidding is not a mistake! This is the way skiers regulate their pace, and a big part of most turns is skidded through by World Cup racers on steep slopes. However, the ski's path is determined by its shape. That means that a carved turn is something that physics can foresee, and this is what the rest of this site does.

02

The hidden circle

Examine a ski from above and see that the sides are curved. It is wider at the tip and tail, thinner in the middle, and each side is a curve of a great circle.

The radius of that circle is called the sidecut radius, Rsc, and is the figure indicated on the ski: I have a slalom ski with a 12 m sidecut radius and my giant-slalom ski has a 30 m sidecut radius.

The curve is only 2 cm deep along a 1.4 m length of the circle's edge, which is in contact with the snow. Use the slider to zoom out so that the entire circle is visible.

Measuring a 12 m circle from a 2 cm curve. I measured the width of each ski every 10 cm along its length and halved it. That gives y, the distance from the centre line to the edge, at each position x along the ski.

Near its narrowest point, a circle of radius R is almost exactly a parabola. From the circle's equation, using √(1 − u) ≈ 1 − u/2 for small u, where u is any number much smaller than 1:

y = R − √(R² − x²) ≈ x²2R

Where this comes from, step by step.

  1. The circle. Put the origin at the narrowest point of the edge, with x along the ski and y measured outwards from there. (The half-width at the middle comes back later, as C in the fit.) The centre of the sidecut circle is a distance R straight out from the origin, at (0, R). Every point of the circle is R from its centre, so Pythagoras gives x² + (y − R)² = R².
  2. Solve for y. (y − R)² = R² − x², so y − R = ±√(R² − x²). Near the origin y is almost 0, far smaller than R, so the minus sign is the right one: y = R − √(R² − x²).
  3. Simplify. Take R out of the square root: √(R² − x²) = R √(1 − u) with u = x²/R². The binomial expansion of (1 − u)1/2 is 1 − u/2 − u²/8 − … On a ski, x is under 1 m and R is over 12 m, so u is less than 1/100 and the first two terms are plenty: y ≈ R − R(1 − x²/(2R²)) = x²/(2R).
  4. The error. The first term left out, u²/8, changes y by only x⁴/(8R³). That is the tiny difference between the circle and the parabola in the next paragraph.

Across a whole ski the difference is under 0.02 mm, far below what a ruler can see. So I fitted y = Ax² + Bx + C to the half-widths. Only A carries the radius: B soaks up any error in where I marked the middle, and C is the half-width there. With y in millimetres and x in metres, y = 1000x²/(2R) = (500/R)x², so:

Rsc = 500A

The x² term is exactly the same: sliding a parabola sideways doesn't change its shape. The mistake only changes the x term, which B takes care of, and the constant, which joins C. That is why only A, the number in front of x², is used for the radius.

Results: SL 12.26 ± 0.09 m (printed 12 m) and GS 28.99 ± 0.39 m (printed 30 m). The fitted curve misses the measurements by only 0.17–0.19 mm on average, less than my ruler's 0.25 mm resolution, so both sides really are circles.

The depth of the curve over the contact length c is its sagitta, s = c²/(8R), where R is the circle's radius. For the SL: 1.406² / (8 × 12.26) = 0.020 m, about 2 cm.

It is the same parabola again. The contact points are at x = ±c/2, so the depth there is y = (c/2)²/(2R) = c²/(8R). Chapter 6 reaches the same formula a second way, with Pythagoras.

03

Tipping flattens the curve

To carve, a skier tips the ski onto its edge by an edge angle, φ. Seen from the snow, a tipped ski looks narrower, and so does its curve. Every distance across the ski shrinks by a factor cos φ, in the same way the shadow of a tilted bow looks straighter than the bow itself.

A flatter curve belongs to a bigger circle. If nothing else happened, tipping would make the turn wider: R = Rsc / cos φ, twice the printed radius at 60°.

Here R is the radius of the turn, Rsc the sidecut radius and φ the edge angle. Why divide by cos φ? A curve's radius depends on how deep it is over its length: the same 1.4 m of edge with half the depth belongs to a circle twice as big. Tipping keeps the length but multiplies the depth by cos φ, so it divides the radius by cos φ. At 60°, cos φ is exactly ½, so the radius doubles.

This matters because it is the opposite of what I feel on snow, where tipping more means turning tighter. It is the prediction to beat: the dashed curve in the results.

But something else does happen.

The sidecut lies in the plane of the ski's base. When the base is tipped by φ, lengths along the ski stay the same, but every distance across it shrinks by a factor cos φ when seen from directly above, which is how the snow "sees" it.

Why cos φ. Take any distance across the base and tip it by φ. It becomes the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle: its shadow on the snow is the side next to the angle, hypotenuse × cos φ, and the side opposite, hypotenuse × sin φ, is how far it rises off the snow. It is the same as splitting a force into two components in mechanics. A line along the ski lies on the axis the ski tips about, so it doesn't change at all.

So a curve of depth s over a chord c becomes a curve of depth s cos φ over the same chord. The sagitta relation from chapter 6, R = c²/(8s), then gives:

Rtipping only = c²8s cos φ = Rsccos φ

Not just the middle. The whole curve changes the same way. From chapter 2, at a distance x along the ski from its narrowest point, the edge has moved out by x²/(2Rsc). Tipped, that becomes x² cos φ/(2Rsc) = x²/(2 · Rsc/cos φ): the same kind of curve, now with radius Rsc/cos φ.

That is 1.15 Rsc at 30°, 1.41 Rsc at 45° and 2 Rsc at 60°. If a tipped ski kept its shape, tipping it further would make every turn wider.

Those three numbers are exact trig values: 1/cos 30° = 2/√3, 1/cos 45° = √2 and 1/cos 60° = 2. Taken to the extreme, a ski standing on its edge at 90° has cos φ = 0 and an infinite radius: seen from above, all of its curve points straight up, and its shadow is a straight line.

04

The ski has to bend

Tipping creates a problem. A tipped ski touches the floor only at the two ends of its curved edge. In between, the edge floats above the floor, like a bridge. On my slalom ski at 60°, the middle of the edge sits 17 mm up.

The skier's weight pushes the middle down, and the ski bends until its whole edge touches. That bend curves the edge far more than tipping flattened it. Push the slider and watch the line on the snow tighten.

Once the edge touches along its full length, pushing harder changes nothing on a hard floor: the floor stops it.

How big is the gap? Each point of the edge is set in from the line between the contact points by its sidecut inset, δ: 20.15 mm at the middle of the SL. Tipping the ski lifts that point by δ sin φ. The ski's camber h, its small built-in arch, adds h cos φ:

gap = δ sin φ + h cos φ

Where the two parts come from.

  1. The sidecut part. Look at the middle of the ski from its end. The inset δ lies flat in the base. Tip the base by φ about the line between the contact points, and δ becomes the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle: δ cos φ across the snow and δ sin φ straight up. The part that goes up is the gap.
  2. The camber part. Camber lifts the middle by h at right angles to the base. Tipping the base by φ also tips that direction φ away from vertical, so the upward part of h is h cos φ. Its sideways part, h sin φ, comes back in chapter 5.
  3. Add the two upward parts and you have the gap.

For the SL at 60.5°: 20.15 × 0.870 + 1.05 × 0.492 = 18.1 mm. I measured 17 mm with a stack of paper.

And a sine is never bigger than 1. So whatever the angle, the gap can never exceed √(δ² + h²): 20.2 mm for the SL and 12.6 mm for the GS. That rule caught one of my measurements (chapter 9).

Why it works. Expand the right-hand side with the compound-angle formula: √(δ² + h²) cos β · sin φ + √(δ² + h²) sin β · cos φ. For this to match the left-hand side, √(δ² + h²) cos β must equal δ and √(δ² + h²) sin β must equal h. A right-angled triangle with sides δ and h does exactly that: its hypotenuse is √(δ² + h²) and its angle β has tan β = h/δ. The biggest gap comes when φ + β = 90°, where the sine is 1.

Why the bend is at right angles to the base. A ski is far thinner than it is wide, so it flexes easily through its thickness and hardly at all sideways. On a tipped ski, "at right angles to the base" is tilted φ from vertical: the same right-angled triangle again, so the bend moves the edge b cos φ down and b sin φ across.

Steeper means much more bend. tan φ grows fast: tan 30° = 1/√3, tan 45° = 1, tan 60° = √3, and it runs off to infinity as φ nears 90°.

05

Bending wins: R = Rsc · cos φ

Put the two effects together. Tipping alone would stretch the turn to Rsc / cos φ, but bending pulls it in so much that the result is

The more you tip a ski, the tighter it turns: 13% tighter than the printed radius at 30°, 29% at 45°, and half at 60°. My 12 m slalom ski carves a 6 m turn at 60°.

On a hard surface nothing else about the ski matters, not its stiffness, length or weight. Only the sidecut and the edge angle.

Follow one point of the edge, set in by δ from the line between the contact points.

  1. Tip the ski by φ. The inset now splits into δ cos φ across the snow and δ sin φ up off the snow, which is the gap.
  2. Bend the ski by b, perpendicular to its base. That moves the point down by b cos φ and across by b sin φ. On a tipped ski, "perpendicular to the base" leans φ away from vertical, like the normal reaction on a slope in mechanics, which is why the bend splits into these two parts. Both "across" parts point the same way, away from the line between the contact points.
  3. The edge touches the snow when the bend cancels the lift: b cos φ = δ sin φ, so b = δ tan φ.
  4. Add up the distance across the snow:

The algebra, slowly. Write tan φ as sin φ / cos φ, so the second term is δ sin²φ / cos φ. Put the first term over the same bottom, δ cos²φ / cos φ, and add the tops. The top is then δ(cos²φ + sin²φ), which is just δ at every angle.

Every inset is multiplied by 1/cos φ, so the curve on the snow is 1/cos φ times deeper. Since R = c²/(8s), a deeper curve over the same chord has a smaller radius, and R = Rsc cos φ.

In symbols: the sagitta s of the flat sidecut becomes s/cos φ on the snow, while the chord c stays the same, so the radius of the turn is R = c² cos φ/(8s) = Rsc cos φ, with Rsc the sidecut radius.

Tipping against bending. Tipping alone multiplies every inset by cos φ (chapter 3); tipping and bending multiply it by 1/cos φ. One gives a turn of Rsc / cos φ, the other Rsc · cos φ. At 60° that is twice the sidecut radius against half of it.

Why camber doesn't matter. If the edge starts lifted by an extra amount ζ (camber, or a raised support), the bend has to cover it too: b = δ tan φ + ζ. The distance across becomes δ cos φ − ζ sin φ + b sin φ = δ/cos φ again. ζ cancels exactly, which is why my loaded measurements don't depend on camber at all.

Where the −ζ sin φ comes from: the extra lift is at right angles to the base, like the camber in chapter 4, so it moves the point ζ cos φ up and ζ sin φ back towards the line between the contact points. The bend then has to push down that extra lift as well, and its extra ζ sin φ across exactly undoes what the lift took away. The ski simply presses its arch flat.

06

Testing it at home

To test the formula I needed the real radius of a tipped, loaded ski. Wooden wedges cut at 30°, 45° and 60° held the ski on its edge, and a phone inclinometer on the ski read the true angle under load.

I pressed the ski down until its edge touched paper taped to the floor along its whole length, then ran a sharp pencil along the edge. The pencil line is the arc the ski would carve.

To find its radius, I measured the straight distance between its ends (the chord, c) and the biggest gap between that line and the arc (the sagitta, s): R = c²/(8s).

Why R = c²/(8s). Join the circle's centre to one end of the chord and to the middle of the arc. Pythagoras gives

  1. Each reading on its own. R goes as c², so a 1% error in the chord makes a 2% error in the radius. R goes as 1/s, so a 1% error in the sagitta makes a 1% error in the radius. With calculus: ln R = 2 ln c − ln s − ln 8, and differentiating gives ΔR/R ≈ 2Δc/c − Δs/s for small changes.
  2. Putting them together. In A-level physics you add the two percentages, 2Δc/c + Δs/s. That is the worst case, with both errors pushing the same way. If the two errors are independent, they are as likely to partly cancel as to add, and the fair combination is the square root of the sum of squares, like the hypotenuse of a triangle whose sides are the two parts. It always comes out a little smaller than the worst case.
  3. An example. For my SL trace at 44°, Δs/s = 0.5/27.6 and 2Δc/c = 2 × 5/1410. The sagitta part is more than twice the chord part, and together they give the ±0.18 m on 9.00 m in the results. The sagitta is only a few centimetres, so half a millimetre is a big fraction of it; the chord is over a metre, so 5 mm hardly matters.

The angle. I read the edge angle φ with the phone at the tip end, the middle and the tail end of the loaded ski. The angle varied by at most 1.3° along the ski, so I used the reading at the middle.

Checking the method. With the ski flat (0°), the pencil traces gave 12.80 ± 0.34 m for the SL and 29.40 ± 1.23 m for the GS, matching the radii from the width measurements in chapter 2. Pencil and ruler agree.

In total: eight traces (two skis at 0°, 30°, 45° and 60°) plus a repeat of the SL at 30°.

08

What it means on the slope

On a slope, the edge angle isn't a free choice. To stay balanced in a turn, a skier leans in, and the faster the turn, the further they have to lean. With the skis tipped as far as the body leans, balance needs tan φ = v²/(gR), where φ is the edge angle, v the speed, g = 9.81 m/s² the pull of gravity and R the radius of the turn. Add R = Rsc cos φ, with Rsc the sidecut radius, and every speed gets exactly one edge angle: v² = g Rsc sin φ.

Holding 45° on my slalom ski means skiing at about 33 km/h, with 1.4 times my body weight on my legs. Since sin φ can't go above 1, a balanced carve has a top speed, √(g Rsc): 40 km/h for the slalom ski and 61 km/h for the giant-slalom ski. That is why GS skis, built for faster turns, have bigger radii.

From 14 years of racing, the ski never stops carving; the skier does. Around 45–50 km/h on slalom skis and 65–70 km/h on GS skis, carving hard throws me off balance. That is a little above the model's limit, and research on World Cup racing explains the gap: racers ski "dynamically", never settling into a balanced lean.

Balance. In a steady turn the snow pushes on the edge. For the skier not to topple, that push has to point straight at their centre of mass, along the body. Its upward part holds up the weight, mg (mass m times gravity g), and its sideways part supplies the force that bends the path into a circle, mv²/R. Their ratio is the lean:

tan φ = v²gR with R = Rsc cos φ ⇒ v² = g Rsc sin φ

Newton's second law for circular motion, resolved up and sideways (steps below). The last step uses tan φ = sin φ / cos φ.

The mechanics, step by step.

  1. Two forces. Treat the skier as a particle at their centre of mass, moving round a horizontal circle of radius R at speed v. Only two forces act: the weight mg straight down, and the push from the snow, F, along the body at the angle φ to the vertical. (Friction along the direction of travel is left out.)
  2. Resolve vertically. The skier neither rises nor sinks, so the upward and downward forces balance: F cos φ = mg.
  3. Resolve horizontally. Moving in a circle needs an acceleration v²/R towards the centre, so by Newton's second law the sideways force is F sin φ = mv²/R.
  4. Divide step 3 by step 2. F and m cancel, leaving tan φ = v²/(gR). The lean doesn't depend on how heavy the skier is.
  5. Put in the ski. With R = Rsc cos φ: sin φ / cos φ = v² / (g Rsc cos φ). Multiply both sides by cos φ and it cancels: sin φ = v²/(g Rsc), which is v² = g Rsc sin φ.

The push from the snow is mg/cos φ: 1.15 times body weight at 30°, 1.41 at 45° and 2 at 60°.

That is step 2 rearranged, F = mg/cos φ, where F is the push from the snow: the load on the legs is 1/cos φ times body weight. The speeds in the table come from the last step. For my SL at 45°, v = √(9.81 × 12.26 × sin 45°) in m/s, and multiplying by 3.6 to turn m/s into km/h gives 33.2 km/h.

The limit. sin φ ≤ 1, so v ≤ √(g Rsc), where v is the speed, g gravity and Rsc the sidecut radius: √(9.81 × 12.26) = 11.0 m/s = 39.5 km/h for the SL, and 60.7 km/h for the GS. Above it the ski still carves, but the turn it cuts is tighter than any lean can hold.

Why racers go faster. The model assumes a steady turn on flat ground with the skier always in balance. Komissarov (2022) found the same limit, √(g Rsc cos α) on a slope of angle α (with g gravity and Rsc the sidecut radius), and showed with World Cup data that racers spend most of each turn above it: their lean swings from side to side and never settles, so they are never in the steady balance the model needs. My own limits are estimates from racing, not measurements.

What went wrong, and the limits

Not everything worked the first time, and the mistakes taught me the most.

  • A prediction that failed

    I expected the ski to twist under load and turn wider than predicted. It twisted by at most 1.3°, and the results show no such trend.

  • The ends of a trace lie

    Pencil lines that ran past the ski's contact points curled inwards and made radii 7.5–15% too large. I predicted this, confirmed it, and from then on measured only between the contact points.

  • A measurement that broke geometry

    One ruler reading put the gap under the GS at 16 mm, but the maths says it can never exceed 12.6 mm. A stack of paper gave 11.5 mm: the ruler had been touching the ski's base, not its edge.

  • One unexplained repeat

    Two traces at the same angle differed by 3.4 mm. I couldn't find why, so I report the average of both.

Limits: a hard floor is not snow, where the edge sinks in; the rig is static, while real turns are dynamic; and the slope is ignored.

Twist. I expected the load to twist the ski so its middle sat at a lower angle than its ends, making the measured radii a few percent too large, more so at steep angles. The phone showed at most 1.3° of twist, largest at 30°, and its direction depended on where I pushed. The deviations from the prediction have no common sign. The prediction was wrong, at least for a static ski on a hard floor.

Chord ends. A sagitta is very sensitive to where the chord ends. Beyond the contact points the edge lifts off the paper, and the last few centimetres of pencil line curl inwards by 2–3 mm. That shrinks the sagitta and makes the radius 7.5–15% too large. Measuring only between the contact points fixed it, and the corrected sagittas came out larger even though the chords were shorter, exactly as predicted.

The impossible reading. With a ruler stood next to the edge, my unloaded gap readings rose further above the prediction the steeper the angle. At 60° the base leans out over the edge, so a ruler a couple of millimetres away touches the base and reads high by about that distance times tan φ, where φ is the edge angle. A paper stack slid under the edge avoids the problem.

The repeat. My two traces of the SL at 30° differ by 3.4 mm in sagitta, far more than the 0.5 mm reading error. One end of the first trace ran 3 mm past a contact point, much too little to explain it. The other traces scatter by only 2.4% around the prediction, so it looks like a one-off, but one repeat can't prove that.

What I would do next. Repeat every trace three times; test on a softer surface that lets the edge sink in, like snow; and measure real turns on snow with a phone's motion sensors and GPS, to compare edge angle, speed and turn radius on the slope.

About this project

I'm Vladislav Zalevskiy, an A-Level student in Belgrade, Serbia, studying Maths, Physics and Computer Science, and a former professional ski racer who competed for 12 years until March of 2026. This project started when I tried my hardest to get behind the radii of different skis, but then I had to ask the question: Does the radius truly stay consistent throughout the full turn or does it change with the force of the skier bending the ski?

Following this, I conducted this experiment at home with my own skis and using my own homemade equipment for it.

How this site was made. The measurements, photos and conclusions are mine. I used Claude, an AI assistant, to check my analysis and build this website.
