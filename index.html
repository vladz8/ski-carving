The Physics of Ski Carving

How does a ski's turning radius change with every turn, and why does a ski turn tighter when you tip it?

A homemade experiment by Vladislav Zalevskiy, with two race skis, some wooden wedges and a pencil.

In 30 seconds

The radius printed on a ski, 12 m on my slalom ski, is the circle its sides are cut from. When a skier tips the ski onto its edge and loads it, the ski bends, and the bend makes it turn tighter: R = Rsc · cos φ, half the printed radius at 60°. I tested this at home on two race skis, and all six measurements agree with the formula to within a few percent.

01

Two ways to turn

A skier can turn in two ways. In a skid, the skis slide sideways across the snow, like a car drifting through a corner. Snow sprays out and speed is lost to friction.

In a carve, the skier tips the skis onto their sharp steel edges, and each edge slices a thin, clean line, like a train following its rails. Racers carve whenever they can, because a ski that isn't sliding sideways isn't wasting speed.

This site is about the carve, and about what decides how tight it is.

In a carve, every part of the edge runs through the same groove in the snow, the way every wheel of a train runs on the same rail. That is why a carved track is two thin lines, one for each ski, while a skid leaves a wide, smeared band.

Skidding is not a mistake. It is how skiers control their speed, and on steep slopes even World Cup racers skid through part of most turns. But in a pure carve, the shape of the ski decides the path. That makes a carved turn something physics can predict, and the rest of this site does exactly that.

02

The hidden circle

Look at a ski from above and its sides are not straight. It is wider at the tip and tail and narrower in the middle, and each side is an arc of a giant circle.

The radius of that circle is called the sidecut radius, Rsc, and it is the number printed on the ski: 12 m on my slalom (SL) ski and 30 m on my giant-slalom (GS) ski.

The circle is so big that along the 1.4 m of edge that touches the snow, the curve is only 2 cm deep. Drag the slider to zoom out until the whole circle appears.

Measuring a 12 m circle from a 2 cm curve. I measured the width of each ski every 10 cm along its length and halved it. That gives y, the distance from the centre line to the edge, at each position x along the ski.

Near its narrowest point, a circle of radius R is almost exactly a parabola. From the circle's equation, using √(1 − u) ≈ 1 − u/2 for small u:

y = R − √(R² − x²) ≈ x²2R

Across a whole ski the difference is under 0.02 mm, far below what a ruler can see. So I fitted y = Ax² + Bx + C to the half-widths. Only A carries the radius: B soaks up any error in where I marked the middle, and C is the half-width there. With y in millimetres and x in metres, y = 1000x²/(2R) = (500/R)x², so:

Rsc = 500A

Results: SL 12.26 ± 0.09 m (printed 12 m) and GS 28.99 ± 0.39 m (printed 30 m). The fitted curve misses the measurements by only 0.17–0.19 mm on average, less than my ruler's 0.25 mm resolution, so both sides really are circles.

The depth of the curve over the contact length c is its sagitta, s = c²/(8R). For the SL: 1.406² / (8 × 12.26) = 0.020 m, about 2 cm.

03

Tipping flattens the curve

To carve, a skier tips the ski onto its edge by an edge angle, φ. Seen from the snow, a tipped ski looks narrower, and so does its curve. Every distance across the ski shrinks by a factor cos φ, in the same way the shadow of a tilted bow looks straighter than the bow itself.

A flatter curve belongs to a bigger circle. If nothing else happened, tipping would make the turn wider: R = Rsc / cos φ, twice the printed radius at 60°.

But something else does happen.

The sidecut lies in the plane of the ski's base. When the base is tipped by φ, lengths along the ski stay the same, but every distance across it shrinks by a factor cos φ when seen from directly above, which is how the snow "sees" it.

So a curve of depth s over a chord c becomes a curve of depth s cos φ over the same chord. The sagitta relation from chapter 6, R = c²/(8s), then gives:

Rtipping only = c²8s cos φ = Rsccos φ

That is 1.15 Rsc at 30°, 1.41 Rsc at 45° and 2 Rsc at 60°. If a tipped ski kept its shape, tipping it further would make every turn wider.

04

The ski has to bend

Tipping creates a problem. A tipped ski touches the floor only at the two ends of its curved edge. In between, the edge floats above the floor, like a bridge. On my slalom ski at 60°, the middle of the edge sits 17 mm up.

The skier's weight pushes the middle down, and the ski bends until its whole edge touches. That bend curves the edge far more than tipping flattened it. Push the slider and watch the line on the snow tighten.

Once the edge touches along its full length, pushing harder changes nothing on a hard floor: the floor stops it.

How big is the gap? Each point of the edge is set in from the line between the contact points by its sidecut inset, δ: 20.15 mm at the middle of the SL. Tipping the ski lifts that point by δ sin φ. The ski's camber c, its small built-in arch, adds c cos φ:

gap = δ sin φ + c cos φ

For the SL at 60.5°: 20.15 × 0.870 + 1.05 × 0.492 = 18.1 mm. I measured 17 mm with a stack of paper.

A check from A-level maths. Any a sin φ + b cos φ can be written as √(a² + b²) · sin(φ + α), and a sine is never bigger than 1. So whatever the angle, the gap can never exceed √(δ² + c²): 20.2 mm for the SL and 12.6 mm for the GS. That rule caught one of my measurements (chapter 9).

How much bend? Bending the ski by b, perpendicular to its base, lowers the edge by b cos φ. Closing the gap needs b cos φ = δ sin φ + c cos φ, so

b = δ tan φ + c

That is about 37 mm at the middle of the SL at 60°. The interactive leaves out the 1 mm camber to keep the picture simple.

05

Bending wins: R = Rsc · cos φ

Put the two effects together. Tipping alone would stretch the turn to Rsc / cos φ, but bending pulls it in so much that the result is

R = Rsc · cos φ

The more you tip a ski, the tighter it turns: 13% tighter than the printed radius at 30°, 29% at 45°, and half at 60°. My 12 m slalom ski carves a 6 m turn at 60°.

On a hard surface nothing else about the ski matters, not its stiffness, length or weight. Only the sidecut and the edge angle.

Follow one point of the edge, set in by δ from the line between the contact points.

  1. Tip the ski by φ. The inset now splits into δ cos φ across the snow and δ sin φ up off the snow, which is the gap.
  2. Bend the ski by b, perpendicular to its base. That moves the point down by b cos φ and across by b sin φ.
  3. The edge touches the snow when the bend cancels the lift: b cos φ = δ sin φ, so b = δ tan φ.
  4. Add up the distance across the snow:
δ cos φ + δ tan φ sin φ = δ cos²φ + sin²φcos φ = δcos φ

Every inset is multiplied by 1/cos φ, so the curve on the snow is 1/cos φ times deeper. Since R = c²/(8s), a deeper curve over the same chord has a smaller radius, and R = Rsc cos φ.

Why camber doesn't matter. If the edge starts lifted by an extra amount ζ (camber, or a raised support), the bend has to cover it too: b = δ tan φ + ζ. The distance across becomes δ cos φ − ζ sin φ + b sin φ = δ/cos φ again. ζ cancels exactly, which is why my loaded measurements don't depend on camber at all.

06

Testing it at home

To test the formula I needed the real radius of a tipped, loaded ski. Wooden wedges cut at 30°, 45° and 60° held the ski on its edge, and a phone inclinometer on the ski read the true angle under load.

I pressed the ski down until its edge touched paper taped to the floor along its whole length, then ran a sharp pencil along the edge. The pencil line is the arc the ski would carve.

To find its radius, I measured the straight distance between its ends (the chord, c) and the biggest gap between that line and the arc (the sagitta, s): R = c²/(8s).

Why R = c²/(8s). Join the circle's centre to one end of the chord and to the middle of the arc. Pythagoras gives

(R − s)² + (c/2)² = R² ⇒ R = c²8s + s2

With s about 2 cm and R about 10 m, the s/2 term is 1 cm in 10 m, under 0.1%, so I dropped it.

How precise. I read the sagitta to ±0.5 mm and the chord to ±5 mm. Because R depends on c²/s, the uncertainties combine as

ΔRR = √( (2Δc/c)² + (Δs/s)² )

which is 2–4% per trace. The sagitta reading is the biggest part.

The angle. I read φ with the phone at the tip end, the middle and the tail end of the loaded ski. The angle varied by at most 1.3° along the ski, so I used the reading at the middle.

Checking the method. With the ski flat (0°), the pencil traces gave 12.80 ± 0.34 m for the SL and 29.40 ± 1.23 m for the GS, matching the radii from the width measurements in chapter 2. Pencil and ruler agree.

In total: eight traces (two skis at 0°, 30°, 45° and 60°) plus a repeat of the SL at 30°.

07

The results

Each point is one pencil trace: the radius it carved divided by the ski's sidecut radius, plotted against the edge angle measured on the ski. The solid curve is the prediction, cos φ. The dashed curve is what tipping alone would give, 1/cos φ.

All six tipped traces sit on the cos φ curve, within their measurement uncertainty, and nowhere near 1/cos φ. At 60° both skis carve about half their sidecut radius: 6.5 m for the slalom ski and 14.4 m for the giant-slalom ski.

A ski turns tighter when you tip it because it bends.

Testing the shape of the law. Suppose the carved radius follows R/Rsc = cosn φ. Tipping plus bending predicts n = +1; tipping alone predicts n = −1. Fitting the six tipped traces gives

n = 1.01 ± 0.02

using the sidecut radii from the width measurements, or n = 1.07 ± 0.04 from the traces alone. The points scatter about as much as their uncertainties say they should (χ² = 3.8 for 6 points).

08

What it means on the slope

On a slope, the edge angle isn't a free choice. To stay balanced in a turn, a skier leans in, and the faster the turn, the further they have to lean. With the skis tipped as far as the body leans, balance needs tan φ = v²/(gR). Add R = Rsc cos φ and every speed gets exactly one edge angle: v² = g Rsc sin φ.

Holding 45° on my slalom ski means skiing at about 33 km/h, with 1.4 times my body weight on my legs. Since sin φ can't go above 1, a balanced carve has a top speed, √(g Rsc): 40 km/h for the slalom ski and 61 km/h for the giant-slalom ski. That is why GS skis, built for faster turns, have bigger radii.

From 14 years of racing, the ski never stops carving; the skier does. Around 45–50 km/h on slalom skis and 65–70 km/h on GS skis, carving hard throws me off balance. That is a little above the model's limit, and research on World Cup racing explains the gap: racers ski "dynamically", never settling into a balanced lean.

Balance. In a steady turn the snow pushes on the edge. For the skier not to topple, that push has to point straight at their centre of mass, along the body. Its upward part holds up the weight, mg, and its sideways part supplies the force that bends the path into a circle, mv²/R. Their ratio is the lean:

tan φ = v²gR with R = Rsc cos φ ⇒ v² = g Rsc sin φ

The push from the snow is mg/cos φ: 1.15 times body weight at 30°, 1.41 at 45° and 2 at 60°.

The limit. sin φ ≤ 1, so v ≤ √(g Rsc): √(9.81 × 12.26) = 11.0 m/s = 39.5 km/h for the SL, and 60.7 km/h for the GS. Above it the ski still carves, but the turn it cuts is tighter than any lean can hold.

Why racers go faster. The model assumes a steady turn on flat ground with the skier always in balance. Komissarov (2022) found the same limit, √(g Rsc cos α) on a slope of angle α, and showed with World Cup data that racers spend most of each turn above it: their lean swings from side to side and never settles, so they are never in the steady balance the model needs. My own limits are estimates from racing, not measurements.

09

What went wrong, and the limits

Not everything worked the first time, and the mistakes taught me the most.

  • A prediction that failed

    I expected the ski to twist under load and turn wider than predicted. It twisted by at most 1.3°, and the results show no such trend.

  • The ends of a trace lie

    Pencil lines that ran past the ski's contact points curled inwards and made radii 7.5–15% too large. I predicted this, confirmed it, and from then on measured only between the contact points.

  • A measurement that broke geometry

    One ruler reading put the gap under the GS at 16 mm, but the maths says it can never exceed 12.6 mm. A stack of paper gave 11.5 mm: the ruler had been touching the ski's base, not its edge.

  • One unexplained repeat

    Two traces at the same angle differed by 3.4 mm. I couldn't find why, so I report the average of both.

Limits: a hard floor is not snow, where the edge sinks in; the rig is static, while real turns are dynamic; and the slope is ignored.

Twist. I expected the load to twist the ski so its middle sat at a lower angle than its ends, making the measured radii a few percent too large, more so at steep angles. The phone showed at most 1.3° of twist, largest at 30°, and its direction depended on where I pushed. The deviations from the prediction have no common sign. The prediction was wrong, at least for a static ski on a hard floor.

Chord ends. A sagitta is very sensitive to where the chord ends. Beyond the contact points the edge lifts off the paper, and the last few centimetres of pencil line curl inwards by 2–3 mm. That shrinks the sagitta and makes the radius 7.5–15% too large. Measuring only between the contact points fixed it, and the corrected sagittas came out larger even though the chords were shorter, exactly as predicted.

The impossible reading. With a ruler stood next to the edge, my unloaded gap readings rose further above the prediction the steeper the angle. At 60° the base leans out over the edge, so a ruler a couple of millimetres away touches the base and reads high by about that distance times tan φ. A paper stack slid under the edge avoids the problem.

The repeat. My two traces of the SL at 30° differ by 3.4 mm in sagitta, far more than the 0.5 mm reading error. One end of the first trace ran 3 mm past a contact point, much too little to explain it. The other traces scatter by only 2.4% around the prediction, so it looks like a one-off, but one repeat can't prove that.

What I would do next. Repeat every trace three times; test on a softer surface that lets the edge sink in, like snow; and measure real turns on snow with a phone's motion sensors and GPS, to compare edge angle, speed and turn radius on the slope.
